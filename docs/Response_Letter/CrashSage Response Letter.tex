\secc{Comment 1:}{The paper correctly identifies the core issue that LLM-generated drivers are untrusted, but does not clearly delineate the specific scope of ``untrusted''. For example, drivers may be maliciously crafted, unintentionally erroneous, or misused due to insufficient API understanding---should attribution strategies differ across these three cases? The authors are advised to distinguish ``malicious drivers'' from ``incompetent drivers'' in the threat model and discuss the applicability of rule-guided crash attribution to these two categories.}
\secr{Response:}{Thank you for pointing out the ambiguity. This study focuses on non-malicious drivers that may be faulty, i.e., LLM-generated fuzz drivers that contain unintended implementation errors or misuse target APIs. The assumption is not that every generated driver is faulty, but that a crash report cannot be taken as evidence of a target defect until the driver has been ruled out. Our goal is to discover defects in target libraries through fuzz testing using these generated drivers. However, the drivers themselves may be faulty, making it necessary to distinguish driver-induced crashes from actual target-library defects. Deliberately malicious drivers are therefore outside the scope of this study. We have clarified this scope in Section~3.1 and stated that rule-guided attribution is designed for unintended driver errors and API misuse.

The above revisions have been incorporated into Section 1 (Introduction), Section 2.1 (LLM-Assisted Fuzzing), and Section 3.1 (LLM-Assisted Fuzzing Framework). The corresponding excerpts are reproduced below.}

\nc{Section 3.1, LLM-Assisted Fuzzing Framework (final paragraph)}{In the workflow described above, generated drivers are assumed to be non-malicious but potentially faulty: they may contain unintentional implementation errors or may misuse the target APIs, and we refer to such a driver as an incompetent driver. The assumption is not that every generated driver is faulty, but that a crash report cannot be taken as evidence of a target defect until the driver has been ruled out. A faulty driver may omit required initialization, construct invalid arguments, or otherwise violate API contracts because of unintentional generation errors or insufficient API understanding. We assume that the sanitizer reports, crash artifacts, and source code used for analysis have not been deliberately tampered with. Under these assumptions, rule-guided crash attribution uses sanitizer signals and stack-frame roles to screen likely driver-induced failures. Deliberately malicious drivers, including those that forge evidence or attempt to evade adjudication, are outside the scope of this work.}

\nc{Section 1, Introduction}{To address this problem, we propose CrashSage, an evidence-guided crash-adjudication pipeline tailored for LLM-assisted fuzzing with incompetent generated drivers.}

\nc{Section 2.1, LLM-Assisted Fuzzing}{Once a generated driver compiles and produces crashes, crash reports are usually treated as fuzzing outcomes rather than as evidence that must be adjudicated under the assumption that the harness may be incompetent.}

\secc{Comment 2:}{Table 8 defines three ground-truth categories---``confirmed target,'' ``confirmed non-target,'' and ``potential target''---but does not specify the number of annotators, inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen's Kappa), or whether annotators were aware of CrashSage's outputs. Given that ``confirmed target'' requires correspondence with public evidence such as CVEs/advisories/patches, and ``potential target'' is excluded from binary metrics, the authors are advised to supplement labeling protocol details and discuss whether excluding 145 potential targets may lead to optimistic estimation of method performance.}
\secr{Response:}{Thank you for this helpful comment on the labeling protocol and the treatment of the 145 potential-target reports. We have replaced the label definitions in the revised manuscript and supplemented the annotation procedure and its statistics.

This study uses two labels, target and non-target. A target label means that the evidence is sufficient to determine that the crash corresponds to a vulnerability in the target library, and a non-target label means that the evidence is sufficient to determine that it does not. The previous three-category scheme, namely ``confirmed target'', ``confirmed non-target'', and ``potential target'', is no longer used, and the earlier requirement that a target label be backed by public evidence has been removed: public vulnerability material and manual verification are two routes to the same evidence rather than a condition on the label. A report without a matching CVE is therefore neither assigned non-target by default nor excluded from the evaluation; it is resolved by manual verification.

The 876 reports of the original submission and the 196 reports added in this revision were labeled by matching each crash to public vulnerability material. For each report, the correspondence was established by checking the target version, the crash signature, the stack trace path, and the triggering input, and a non-target label was determined from the verification of the relevant code and of the triggering process rather than from the mere absence of a matching CVE.

For the 145 reports that lacked sufficient public vulnerability evidence, we conducted an additional manual audit. Three people with C/C++ memory-safety analysis experience took part. These 145 reports fall into 32 groups under the representative mapping produced by representative selection, and the annotation unit was the group: the two reviewers reproduced the representative crash from the triggering input, checked the crash signature and the stack trace path, and, using the API contracts and the relevant source code, judged whether the crash exposes a vulnerability in the target library, without seeing CrashSage outputs. Reviewer A labeled 31 groups and Reviewer B labeled all 32, so 31 groups carry paired labels; the reviewers agreed on 22 of them, which gives a raw agreement of 70.97\% (22 of 31 groups) and a Cohen's kappa of 0.413. The unit of these two statistics is the representative group and the denominator is the 31 groups with paired labels, not the 145 reports, because a group was judged as one analysis unit. The third person re-examined the nine disagreement groups and the one unpaired group, reviewed the basis of both annotations, and performed the necessary code inspection and reproduction to determine the final label. Each group was labeled only after the reviewers checked the member reports listed for that group, and a group whose members did not share the same causal path had to be flagged and split; no group required splitting. The group label was then applied to its members, and all 26 reports that finally received a target label were additionally verified one by one against their own crash evidence, so the target labels do not rest on the grouping alone. The ground truth for this subset is therefore established at group granularity: the 119 non-target reports were not annotated individually, so a member that differs from its representative in input or object state would not be separated by this protocol. In total, 26 reports were labeled target and 119 were labeled non-target.

The 876 reports of the original submission, the 196 reports added in this revision, and the 145 audited reports therefore account for all 1,217 reports. The revised evaluation includes all of them, comprising 215 target and 1,002 non-target reports, and no report is excluded from the binary metrics. All experimental configurations use this same fixed ground truth, so the previously pending reports are counted rather than set aside. We have added the label definitions, the annotation procedure, and the sample statistics to Section~\ref{paper-subsec:experimental-setup}.

The above revisions have been incorporated into Section 5.1 (Experimental Setup) and Table 8. The corresponding excerpts are reproduced below.}

\secc{Comment 3:}{Section 4.3 performs representative selection via exact and stack signatures, and Section 5.3 states that ``each representative diagnosis and ground-truth label are propagated to its duplicate reports before computing TP, FP, and FN.'' However, the transitivity of signature matching (i.e., whether A matches B and B matches C implies A matches C) and the risk of error propagation (a misjudged representative causing the entire group to be misjudged) are not discussed. The authors are advised to supplement: (1) the equivalence class construction algorithm for signature matching; (2) case analysis or quantitative evaluation of group-level misjudgment caused by representative misjudgment.}
\secr{Response:}{Thank you for this careful reading. We have clarified the representative-selection algorithm, corrected the description of label propagation, and added an analysis of the effect of representative misjudgment.

First, during model inference and metric computation, only the representative prediction is propagated to its associated reports. The ground-truth labels established under the annotation protocol remain fixed and are not modified by the representative predictions, and each propagated prediction is evaluated against the corresponding report's recorded label, so errors introduced by propagation enter the final metrics. The statement on ground-truth propagation in the previous version has been corrected.

Second, Algorithm~1 uses a representative-based matching strategy rather than constructing strict equivalence classes through a transitive closure. The algorithm compares each report, in discovery order, with the existing representatives by exact or stack signature; when the matching condition is satisfied, the report is associated with the first matching representative, and otherwise it becomes a new representative. Reports are therefore not merged merely because of an indirect match, and the procedure does not require the matching relation to be transitive. Representativeness follows from the signature-matching condition, and ``first match'' is used only to determine a unique mapping.

Third, to quantify the effect of representative misjudgment, we flip the prediction of each of the 96 representatives in turn and recompute the report-level metrics from the saved mapping. The maximum absolute change in overall F1 is 0.0174, and in the most influential case F1 changes from 0.8379 to 0.8205. The largest group contains seven reports, so a single flip changes at most seven of the 1,217 report-level decisions. A change in one representative affects only its own group, while the other groups remain unchanged, so the overall effect on this dataset is limited. Representative selection also reduces LLM submissions by 82.02\%, and in this evaluation V1 and V2 achieve identical report-level attribution metrics. We have added these clarifications to the revised manuscript.

The above revisions have been incorporated into Section 4.3 (Representative Selection) and Section 5.4 (Evaluation of Representative Selection). The corresponding excerpts are reproduced below.}

\nc{Section 4.3, Representative Selection}{CrashSage processes the retained crashes in discovery order and compares each crash with the representatives selected so far. A matched crash is linked to the corresponding representative, whereas an unmatched crash becomes a new representative. Algorithm~\ref{paper-alg:representative-selection} summarizes this procedure.

The procedure therefore does not construct equivalence classes through a transitive closure. A crash is associated with the first matching representative, and two crashes that match the same representative are not thereby declared equivalent. Each crash is mapped to at most one representative, and the mapping is stored explicitly rather than inferred from the match relation.}

\nc{Section 5.4, Evaluation of Representative Selection}{Figure~\ref{paper-fig:representative-counts} shows that V1 submits all 534 reports retained after rule-guided crash attribution to the LLM, whereas V2 submits only 96 representatives and removes 438 repeated or redundant reports. Representative selection therefore reduces LLM submissions by 82.02\% relative to V1. During inference, the prediction of each representative is propagated through the saved mapping. Ground-truth labels remain fixed, and TP, FP, TN, and FN are computed against the recorded report-level labels. In this evaluation, V1 and V2 achieve identical report-level attribution metrics, while V2 requires substantially fewer LLM submissions. Although each V2 request contains richer analysis content than an individual raw report, reducing the number of LLM submissions from 534 in V1 to 96 in V2 substantially lowers the overall LLM invocation cost as measured by the number of LLM submissions.}

\nc{Section 5.4, Evaluation of Representative Selection}{To evaluate group-level error propagation, we flip each of the 96 representative predictions individually and recompute the report-level metrics from the saved mapping and the archived V4 predictions. Over the 96 flips, the maximum absolute F1 change is 0.0174, and the worst case reduces F1 from 0.8379 to 0.8205. The largest group contains seven reports, so a single flip changes at most seven of the 1,217 report-level decisions.}

\secc{Comment 5:}{Table 7 defines V0 as the ``CKGFuzzer baseline,'' but CKGFuzzer is itself a fuzz driver generation system whose crash adjudication capability is not its design goal. Is it fair to use it as a crash adjudication baseline? The authors are advised to supplement: (1) comparative experiments with dedicated crash deduplication/localization methods (e.g., Igor, ReBucket, CrashLocator); (2) comparison with a baseline that directly uses LLM to classify raw reports; (3) discussion of why CKGFuzzer was chosen over other LLM-assisted fuzzing systems (e.g., PromptFuzz, Fuzz4All) as the baseline.}
\secr{Response:}{Thank you for this valuable comment. The comment raises three points: whether CKGFuzzer is a fair crash-adjudication baseline, which dedicated methods and which raw-report baseline the comparison should include, and why CKGFuzzer was chosen over other LLM-assisted fuzzing systems. We address them in turn.

On the fairness of the baseline, CKGFuzzer enters the comparison as the complete crash-cause analysis pipeline rather than as a driver generator alone. Its crash-analysis stage is active in V0, so the comparison does not credit CrashSage with adjudication that the baseline already performs. All variants share the same upstream driver generation and fuzzing workflow and the same crash reports, and the revised manuscript identifies which CKGFuzzer components are retained and which components are added. The V0--V4 comparison therefore measures the four added stages on top of that pipeline.

On dedicated methods, we select ReBucket as the external reference for the selection stage, because it clusters crashes by call-stack similarity, which is directly related to reducing repeated analysis, and it can be applied to the same report set. On the 534 reports retained after rule-guided crash attribution, ReBucket selects 103 cluster representatives and CrashSage selects 96, corresponding to reductions of 80.71\% and 82.02\%, and the pairwise Jaccard agreement is 0.9824.

On the raw-report LLM baseline, we pass each of the 1,217 reports directly to the same model with a minimal direct instruction, without filtering, representative selection, reconstructed context, or the differential prompt. It performs the same binary decision as CrashSage and obtains Precision 0.2010, Recall 0.5488, and F1 0.2943, against 0.7654, 0.9256, and 0.8379 for CrashSage.

On the choice of CKGFuzzer, it provides the driver generation, fuzzing, and crash-analysis workflow on which this study builds. PromptFuzz and Fuzz4All target input or driver generation and do not expose the crash-analysis interface used here. Replacing CKGFuzzer with either system would change the crash corpus, the build and sanitizer settings, and the labeling conditions, so the comparison would conflate upstream fuzzing effectiveness with downstream adjudication quality.

The above revisions have been incorporated into Section 5.1 (Experimental Setup), Section 5.2 (Comparison with Raw-Only LLM Analysis), and Table 7. The corresponding excerpts are reproduced below.}

Both configurations use the same model, the same temperature, and the same output schema. Rule-guided filtering removes 683 reports, all of which are non-target, and retains all 215 target reports.
